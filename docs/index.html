Beyond GDP.
What really drives national happiness?

The World Happiness Report has crowned Finland the happiest country on earth for seven years running. But why? National wealth is the obvious suspect, yet Afghanistan sits at the bottom of the ranking with a twentieth of US income. Money cannot be the whole story.

This piece pulls together three live data sources, integrates them in a small SQL database, fits both classical OLS and a modern causal forest, and asks: once we control for everything else, when does income still buy happiness?

1 · The headlines

Finland's nearly six-point lead

Finland's Ladder score sits four times above Afghanistan's. The Cantril Ladder question (a 0–10 scale of life satisfaction) is the same instrument worldwide, so the gap is comparable. A six-point gap on a ten-point scale is enormous; any explanation has to grapple with it.

2 · Money matters — with diminishing returns

Doubling income from $2k to $4k buys the same Ladder bump as doubling from $32k to $64k

Plotting the Ladder score against GDP per capita on a log axis gives a clear upward relationship. The smoother bends noticeably above around $30,000 PPP. Costa Rica out-ranks the United States despite earning less than half of US income, a small-scale version of the Easterlin paradox.

Two things complicate the simple "money buys happiness" reading. The curve clearly bends, exactly what we would expect from logarithmic utility. And the vertical scatter is wide: countries with similar income levels routinely differ by close to two Ladder points. That residual variation is where this post lives.

3 · Decomposing the Ladder

What carries the rich-country premium?

The WHR ships six explanatory variables alongside the Ladder. To see how each one contributes to the gap above the world average, I fit a plain OLS regression of the Ladder on the six and then multiply each country's covariate (centred on the sample mean) by the fitted coefficient.

Social support is the third-richest contributor

The blue bar (the share of respondents saying they have someone to count on in tough times) is the single largest contributor in almost every Nordic country. Income comes third.

Freedom is universal

Yellow is large and stable across all 15. The highest-ranked country with low freedom is Israel (4th), which makes up the difference through unusually strong social support and life expectancy.

Trust does the heavy lifting

The gap between the world average and Finland's score is mostly not bought with money. The pattern lines up with Robert Putnam's social-capital research and Bo Rothstein's Nordic "high-trust equilibrium" account.

5 · A causal forest

When does income still buy happiness?

Cross-country comparisons aren't a randomised trial; rich and poor countries differ on dozens of margins at once. To probe heterogeneity flexibly I fit a causal forest (Wager & Athey, 2018) with econml.dml.CausalForestDML, mirroring the recipe Damian uses in immigrantEffects.py. The "treatment" is being above the median income; X covers six potential moderators.

The average treatment effect is essentially zero once the moderators are controlled for. Almost all of the apparent income premium is mediated by them, but the conditional effects do vary.

Internet penetration is the single most important moderator. Once we know whether a country's residents are online, the leftover role of GDP per capita shrinks sharply, an echo of the digital-connectivity literature and the WHR 2026 edition's own pivot to social-media research.

6 · A bonus from the scrape

The Report itself is changing

The rankings spreadsheets are the core of the analysis, but the chapter metadata I scraped from the seven WHR editions has its own story. Across 2020–2026 I collected 51 chapters with their author lists, affiliations and reading times — a tidy table that does not exist anywhere on the WHR website itself.

Chapter counts are volatile (2024 was unusually slim with five chapters), but average reading time has risen sharply: roughly 27 minutes in 2020 to 40 in 2024. The Report is, quite literally, a longer read.

The very top of the ranking is essentially flat (Finland barely moves), but the time series show the slow erosion of US scores relative to Northern Europe and a small post-pandemic recovery in parts of Latin America.

7 · Take-aways

Five things I take from this

  1. Money matters, but mostly indirectly. Once you control for social support, freedom, life expectancy and trust, the marginal contribution of income to subjective well-being is small.
  2. The income–happiness link is heterogeneous, not monotone. The causal forest produces CATEs roughly between −0.16 and +0.15, but they don't line up neatly with how rich a country is.
  3. Social capital is at least as potent as material wealth. In every Nordic country the biggest single contributor to the Ladder gap above the world mean is social support, not GDP.
  4. Internet penetration is the dominant moderator. Split-importance ranks it ahead of social support, corruption, life expectancy, freedom and urbanisation.
  5. The Report itself is changing. Chapters have got longer and the 2026 edition pivots sharply onto social media. The scrape lets us watch that shift in real time.

Replication

One command rebuilds everything

Every figure on this page is generated by a Python pipeline tracked under git. Clone, install, run: 

git clone https://github.com/maksimkitikov/Data-Science-in-Economics.git
cd Data-Science-in-Economics
pip install -r requirements.txt
make all

The Makefile walks the eight numbered scripts in src/, regenerates the SQLite database, the OLS table and the figures, executes the Jupyter notebook and re-renders this page.
