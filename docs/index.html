Beyond GDP.
What really drives national happiness?

The World Happiness Report has crowned Finland the happiest country on earth for seven years running. But why? National wealth is the obvious suspect, yet Afghanistan sits at the bottom of the ranking with under 3% of US income per head. Money cannot be the whole story.

This piece pulls together three live data sources, integrates them in a small SQL database, fits both classical OLS and a modern causal forest, and asks: once we control for everything else, when does income still buy happiness?

1 · The headlines

Finland's nearly six-point lead

Finland's Ladder score sits four times above Afghanistan's. The Cantril Ladder question (a 0-10 scale of life satisfaction) is the same instrument worldwide, so the gap is comparable. A six-point gap on a ten-point scale is enormous; any explanation has to grapple with it.

    2 · Money matters - with diminishing returns

    Below $30k PPP each doubling of income buys roughly the same Ladder bump; above it, the curve flattens

    Plotting the Ladder score against GDP per capita on a log axis gives a clear upward relationship. The smoother bends noticeably above around $30,000 PPP. Costa Rica nearly matches the United States on the Ladder (6.6 versus 6.9) on roughly a third of US income, a small-scale version of the Easterlin paradox.

    The vertical scatter is also wide: countries with similar income levels routinely differ by close to two Ladder points. That residual variation is where this post lives.

    3 · Decomposing the Ladder

    What carries the rich-country premium?

    The WHR ships six explanatory variables alongside the Ladder. To see how each one contributes to the gap above the world average, I fit a plain OLS regression of the Ladder on the six and then multiply each country's covariate (centred on the sample mean) by the fitted coefficient.

    Social support, not income, leads

    The blue bar (the share of respondents saying they have someone to count on in tough times) is the single largest contributor in almost every Nordic country. Income comes third.

    Freedom is universal

    Yellow is large and stable across all 15. The highest-ranked country with low freedom is Israel (4th), which makes up the difference through unusually strong social support and life expectancy.

    Trust does the heavy lifting

    The gap between the world average and Finland's score is mostly not bought with money. The pattern lines up with Robert Putnam's social-capital research and Bo Rothstein's Nordic "high-trust equilibrium" account.

    All specifications use Robust (HC3) standard errors. (m6) is fitted on z-scored variables. (m5) swaps WHR's bundled log-GDP for the World Bank's PPP series as a cross-source sanity check. N is 134 because two countries are missing the corruption variable.

    The log-GDP coefficient drops from 0.80 on its own to 0.27 once social support, freedom and corruption are controlled for. Most of the apparent income premium runs through those other channels.

    The pattern is consistent with a long line of work in the economics of well-being. Easterlin (1974) first noted that within rich countries the income-happiness relationship flattens after basic needs are met; later work (Stevenson & Wolfers, 2008; Layard, Mayraz & Nickell, 2009) finds the gradient survives in cross-country data but is much smaller once mediating institutions are accounted for. Specification (6) makes the size comparable: a one-standard-deviation rise in social support is worth roughly the same as a one-standard-deviation rise in log GDP, and freedom is not far behind.

    Specification (5) is a sanity check: replacing WHR's bundled log GDP with the independent World Bank PPP series leaves the coefficient almost unchanged (0.27 vs 0.26), so the headline does not depend on which income series we use.

    5 · A causal forest

    When does income still buy happiness?

    Cross-country comparisons aren't a randomised trial; rich and poor countries differ on dozens of margins at once. To probe heterogeneity flexibly I fit a causal forest (Wager & Athey, 2018) with econml.dml.CausalForestDML. The "treatment" is being above the median income; X covers six potential moderators. The forest is fitted on n = 130: six of the 136 countries fall out because their internet- or urban-share data is missing.

    ATE ... Ladder points · 95 % CI: ...

    The average treatment effect is essentially zero once the moderators are controlled for. Almost all of the apparent income premium is mediated by them, but the conditional effects do vary. The distribution of CATEs below is the main result for this section; the next two charts and the robustness table are diagnostics that probe the same number from different angles.

    Diagnostics

    Two follow-up plots probe the same number from different angles: where in the GDP distribution the CATE varies, and which moderators are doing the work inside the forest.

    Internet penetration is the single most important moderator. Once we know whether a country's residents are online, the leftover role of GDP per capita shrinks sharply, an echo of the digital-connectivity literature and the WHR 2026 edition's own pivot to social-media research.

    Robustness check: where exactly is "rich"?

    The "above median" treatment is one defensible choice; the natural alternative is the top quartile. Refitting with a 75th-percentile cut-off (around $44k PPP) actually flips the headline: the ATE rises to about +0.32 Ladder points with a 95 % CI clear of zero. So the median split was hiding a real income effect at the very top of the distribution - being rich-rich does buy a small but measurable happiness premium even once moderators are accounted for.

    6 · A bonus from the scrape

    The Report itself is changing

    The rankings spreadsheets are the core of the analysis, but the chapter metadata I scraped from the seven WHR editions has its own story. Across 2020-2026 I collected 51 chapters with their author lists, affiliations and reading times - a tidy table that does not exist anywhere on the WHR website itself.

    Chapter counts are volatile (2024 was unusually slim with five chapters), but average reading time has risen sharply: roughly 27 minutes in 2020 to 40 in 2024. The Report is, quite literally, a longer read.

    The very top of the ranking is essentially flat (Finland barely moves), but the time series show the slow erosion of US scores relative to Northern Europe and a small post-pandemic recovery in parts of Latin America.

    7 · Take-aways

    Five things I take from this

    1. Money matters, but mostly indirectly. Once you control for social support, freedom, life expectancy and trust, the marginal contribution of income to subjective well-being is small. This is the modern empirical reading of the Easterlin paradox: GDP shows up in well-being not because cash is happiness, but because richer countries tend to have the institutions that produce it.
    2. The link is non-linear and concentrated at the top. Splitting at the median gives an ATE indistinguishable from zero, but moving the cut to the 75th percentile (~$44k PPP) lifts it to about +0.32 Ladder points with a 95 % CI clear of zero. So the income effect has not vanished, it has migrated into the richest tail of the distribution.
    3. Social capital is at least as potent as material wealth. In every Nordic country the biggest single contributor to the Ladder gap above the world mean is social support, not GDP.
    4. Internet penetration is the dominant moderator. Split-importance ranks it ahead of social support, corruption, life expectancy, freedom and urbanisation.
    5. The Report itself is changing. Chapters have got longer and the 2026 edition pivots sharply onto social media. The scrape lets us watch that shift in real time.

    Replication

    One command rebuilds everything

    Every figure on this page is generated by a Python pipeline tracked under git. Clone, install, run: 

    git clone https://github.com/maksimkitikov/Data-Science-in-Economics.git
cd Data-Science-in-Economics
pip install -r requirements.txt
make all

    The Makefile walks the nine numbered scripts in src/, regenerates the SQLite database, the OLS table and the figures, executes the Jupyter notebook and re-renders this page.
